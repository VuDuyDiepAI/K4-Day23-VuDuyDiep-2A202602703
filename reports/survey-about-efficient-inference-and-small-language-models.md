# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: Compression, Serving Systems, and the Evidence Behind the Claims

## TL;DR

- Efficient inference has consolidated into three complementary levers — compressing the model (quantization, pruning, distillation), re-engineering the serving stack (memory management, attention kernels, scheduling), and accelerating decoding (speculation and early exit) — and the largest reported gains usually come from combining them rather than from any single technique [1][2][3][4].
- Quantization is close to free at 8-bit weights/activations or 4-bit weights with 16-bit activations, but below that the evidence is genuinely contested: one systematic study reports lossless W8A8/W4A16 and no output-length inflation [5], while another measures drops of up to 69.81% on hard math and a third finds that low-bit models inflate their reasoning-token counts enough to erase per-token speedups [6][7].
- Small language models (roughly 0.1B–5B parameters) now match models 2–5× larger on many benchmarks, driven mainly by data curation, synthetic "textbook" data, heavy overtraining and distillation from larger teachers [8][9][10][11].
- That strategy has a stated boundary: distillation is only more efficient than plain supervised training below a student-size-dependent threshold and when a suitable teacher already exists, and a stronger teacher can produce a worse student [12]; independent small-model benchmarking also finds scale is not the sole driver of reasoning ability [11].
- Efficiency headline numbers are mostly author-reported and best-case. Independent re-evaluations find that KV-cache memory savings often do not become throughput or latency savings [3], that compression can silently destroy instruction-following [13], and that energy per token and total request energy can move in opposite directions [14].

## Background

Transformer inference splits into two phases with opposite hardware profiles. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel and is compute-bound; autoregressive decoding issues matrix–vector products that are memory-bound and leave compute idle. Recent hardware–software co-design work states the mechanism explicitly: "the autoregressive decoding phase is dominated by GEMV operations that are inherently memory-bound, resulting in poor utilization and prohibitive energy costs at the edge" [15]. The 2024 low-bit survey reaches the same conclusion from the compression side, observing that for LLMs the bottleneck is "data transmission and storage costs", which is exactly why weight-only quantization is attractive [16]. Classic IO-aware attention design addressed the same problem from the kernel side, reducing HBM reads and writes through tiling [17]. A practical consequence is that a memory saving is not a latency saving; this distinction recurs throughout the literature reviewed below [3][14].

Efficiency work sits inside a broader literature that frames large-model research around pre-training, adaptation tuning, utilization and capacity evaluation [18], but the practical constraints are different: adaptation, serving and evaluation all consume compute that the efficiency literature tries to reduce.

"Small language model" is a moving target rather than a fixed size class. The dedicated SLM survey restricts itself to transformer-based, decoder-only models of 100M–5B parameters, noting that as of September 2024 7B models were still mostly deployed in the cloud, while also warning that "the definition of 'small' could drift over time, considering that device memory is increasing" [19]. A parallel survey makes the point historically: GPT-2 at 1.5B was a "large language model" in 2019 but is smaller than many models covered as small today [20]. This survey therefore treats "small" as a deployment claim (capability per unit of memory, latency and energy) rather than a parameter count, and it treats the efficiency literature as three interacting layers: compression [21][22][16], systems [1][23][2][24], and decoding [25][4][26].

## Compression: quantization, pruning and distillation

**Post-training quantization (PTQ) became practical because of outliers.** Early LLM quantization had to contend with emergent large-magnitude activation outliers. LLM.int8() isolated those outlier dimensions — roughly 0.1% of feature dimensions — into a 16-bit path while multiplying more than 99.9% of values in 8-bit, halving memory and reducing BLOOM-176B's footprint by 1.96× with no reported degradation; notably, outliers only appear from roughly the 6.7B scale upward, and the method could slow inference for models below that [27]. GPTQ then made one-shot weight quantization cheap: an approximate second-order method quantizing 175B models to 3–4 bits in about four GPU hours, with a 0.03 perplexity loss at 4-bit on the 175B model against 2.2 for round-to-nearest (a comparison stated in the paper's body rather than its abstract, so it could not be recovered from an abstract-level re-check) [28]. Its reported speedups (3.25× on A100, 4.5× on A6000 in the arXiv version; about 2×/4× in the ICLR camera-ready) illustrate a recurring reproducibility hazard, since the same paper is quoted with different numbers depending on version; the authors also state that the gains come from reduced memory movement and do not reduce computation, and they do not cover activation quantization [28]. AWQ took a different route by protecting only the ~1% of salient channels identified from activation statistics, reporting 3.2–3.3× measured speedups over HuggingFace FP16 in TinyChat and roughly 4× lower memory, including deployment of a 70B Llama-2 on a Jetson Orin 64GB [29]. SmoothQuant attacked activations directly by migrating quantization difficulty from activations to weights with a mathematically equivalent transformation, enabling W8A8 with up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction and letting OPT-175B be served on four rather than eight GPUs [30].

**Later work removed the remaining outliers rather than isolating them.** QuaRot applies randomized Hadamard rotations so that weights, activations and KV cache can all be quantized to 4 bits end-to-end, reporting at most 0.47 WikiText-2 perplexity loss with 99% of zero-shot performance retained on Llama 2-70B, plus 3.33× prefill speedup and 3.89× decoding memory saving on the same model [31]. OmniQuant keeps PTQ-level cost while learning its quantization parameters, reporting average W4A4 accuracy gains of 4.99–11.80% and a case where LLaMA-13B at W2A16 reaches 13.21 perplexity where GPTQ rises to 3832 [32]. Quantization-aware training (QAT) remains the escape hatch at very low bit-width: LLM-QAT is data-free, quantizes weights, activations and the KV cache, and in a 4-8-4 configuration lands 1.5 points below full precision where PTQ methods "produce poor results" [33]. These methods are best read as a taxonomy rather than a leaderboard; the 2021 quantization survey still supplies the standard PTQ-versus-QAT framing and the observation that sub-4-bit representations promise up to 16× memory and latency reductions but that 4–8× is what is usually realized in practice [22], while the 2024 low-bit survey organizes the field by bit-width of parameters, activations and gradients, and by equivalents transformations and weight compensation [16]. Fine-tuning on quantized weights is a related but distinct lever: QLoRA backpropagates through a frozen 4-bit NormalFloat base into low-rank adapters, cutting 65B fine-tuning memory from over 780GB to under 48GB on a single GPU [34]. The compression landscape is also surveyed directly, covering quantization, pruning and distillation together with benchmarking strategies [21].

**Pruning and distillation converge on the same lesson: cheap criteria work at scale, but small models are harder.** SparseGPT pruned GPT-family models to at least 50% sparsity in one shot with no retraining, sparsifying 175B models in under 4.5 hours, and reported that one-shot magnitude pruning collapses beyond about 30% sparsity where SparseGPT reaches 60% [35]. Wanda replaced the expensive reconstruction with a per-output importance score combining weight magnitude and input activation norm, requiring a single forward pass and no weight update; on LLaMA-7B at 50% unstructured sparsity it reported perplexity 7.26 versus 17.29 for magnitude pruning, while noting that SparseGPT remained better for structured 2:4 sparsity on small models and Wanda for larger ones [36]. Both papers flag that accuracy is weaker on smaller models, which is precisely where the small-model literature needs compression most. Structured pruning targets real hardware speedups rather than sparsity statistics, and hardware-aware methods in this family claim compression and speedup without accuracy loss relative to pruning and distillation baselines, though the retrieved evidence for one representative method is summary-level only [37]. Distillation spans generations: DistilBERT reduced BERT by 40% in size while retaining 97% of language understanding and being 60% faster [38]; TinyBERT's transformer-specific, two-stage distillation reported a 7.5× smaller and 9.4× faster model at over 96.8% of BERT-base GLUE performance [39]; MobileBERT targeted devices directly at 4.3× smaller and 5.5× faster than BERT-base with a 62 ms latency on a Pixel 4 phone [40]; and intermediate-layer "patient" distillation established that matching several hidden layers, not just logits, is a viable compression signal [41]. At LLM scale, MiniLLM argued that reversing the Kullback–Leibler direction prevents the student from overestimating the teacher's low-probability regions, and reported better instruction-following and calibration than standard distillation across students from 120M to 13B parameters [42]. Architectural efficiency is a fourth, parallel tradition — the efficient-transformers survey catalogues attention variants by complexity and use case [43] — and the compression surveys position all of these as one optimization space alongside benchmarking and evaluation practices [21][16].

## Serving systems: memory, kernels and scheduling

**KV cache memory management was the first big systems win.** PagedAttention partitions each sequence's KV cache into fixed-size blocks, borrowing virtual-memory paging to make sharing within and across requests cheap, and the resulting vLLM system reported 2–4× higher throughput than FasterTransformer and Orca at the same latency, with the advantage growing for longer sequences, larger models and more complex decoding [1]. Continuous batching, credited in the vLLM-era literature to Orca's iteration-level scheduling, is the scheduling half of that story; a vendor benchmark reported up to 23× throughput improvement when continuous batching is combined with vLLM-style memory optimizations, while also showing that naive static batching collapses to 81 tokens/s when sequence lengths vary [44]. Because this is a vendor blog without a stated GPU in the retrieved text, the 23× figure should be treated as an upper-bound illustration rather than a controlled measurement [44].

**Kernels and reuse patterns target the prefill/decode asymmetry.** FlashAttention is exact rather than approximate, using tiling to minimize HBM traffic; its reported training gains were 15% on BERT-large, 3× on GPT-2 and 2.4× on long-range arena at 1K–4K sequence length [17]. FlashAttention-2 addressed work partitioning, reporting roughly 2× over FlashAttention-1 and 50–73% of A100 peak FLOPs/s [23]; FlashAttention-3 then reported that FlashAttention-2 reached only about 35% utilization on H100 and used warp-specialization and FP8 support to reach 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 (75% utilization) and close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8, with 1.5–2.0× speedup over FlashAttention-2 and 2.6× lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention [45]. Multi-call workloads exploit prefix reuse instead: SGLang's RadixAttention reported up to 6.4× higher throughput on structured programs such as agents, RAG pipelines and multi-turn chat, where gains come from prefix reuse rather than single-shot generation [46], and Prompt Cache precomputed reusable prompt segments to cut time-to-first-token by 8× on GPU and 60× on CPU [47]. The production counterpart is automatic prefix caching, implemented in vLLM with hash-based block reuse and documented as "almost a free lunch and won't change model outputs" — with the explicit caveat that it only reduces prefill and does nothing for decoding, and with documented multi-tenant risks that motivate per-request cache salts [48].

**Scheduling research now attacks the prefill/decode interference directly.** Sarathi-Serve introduced chunked prefill, splitting prompts into near-equal chunks so that new requests can be admitted without pausing ongoing decodes, reporting 2.6× higher serving capacity for Mistral-7B on one A100, 3.7× for Yi-34B on two A100s and up to 5.6× end-to-end serving capacity for Falcon-180B with pipeline parallelism, all against vLLM [2]. DistServe took the more radical step of disaggregating the two phases onto different GPUs entirely, co-optimizing resource allocation and parallelism per phase and reporting that it can serve 7.4× more requests or meet a 12.6× tighter SLO within latency constraints for over 90% of requests [24]. Note that these are goodput and tail-latency claims, not raw throughput claims, and that they are measured against a specific baseline [2][24].

**KV-cache compression is the most contested area of the field.** H2O exploited the observation that a small fraction of tokens ("heavy hitters") dominates attention scores, evicting on a dynamic submodular objective and reporting up to 29× throughput improvement over DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and HuggingFace Accelerate [49]. StreamingLLM identified the "attention sink" phenomenon and showed that keeping a few initial tokens lets finite-window models generalize to sequences of up to 4M tokens with no fine-tuning, outperforming a sliding-window recomputation baseline by up to 22.2× [50]. KIVI quantized the cache asymmetrically — per-channel for keys, per-token for values — to 2 bits with a hardware-friendly layout, reporting 2.6× less peak memory (including weights) and 2.35–3.47× throughput on real workloads through larger batches [51]. For long contexts, MInference selects among A-shape, vertical-slash and block-sparse patterns per head and builds sparse indices at inference time, reporting up to 10× prefill latency reduction on an A100 and noting that an 8B model otherwise needs about 30 minutes on a single A100 to prefill a 1M-token prompt, with up to 96.8% attention sparsity at 128K context [52]. These are large, author-reported numbers, and two re-evaluations complicate them. A 2025 serving-oriented re-evaluation found that despite reduced memory, current implementations are not optimized for production serving, giving suboptimal throughput, and that compression "may lead to longer outputs, resulting in increased end-to-end latency"; per-sample analysis also revealed task-specific failures invisible in aggregate averages [3]. A separate study of five compression methods on Llama3.1 8B found that under multi-instruction prompting "certain instructions degrade much more rapidly with compression, effectively causing them to be completely ignored by the LLM", while conceding that "the gains in throughput are indisputable" [13]. Two 2026 preprints extend the same skepticism to newer settings: chunked KV-cache compression exhibits "phase sensitivity", where the same information is easy to retrieve at one window phase and difficult at another [53], and quantizing the fixed-size recurrent states of linear-attention models propagates errors across decoding steps, a case where the memory bottleneck is the state rather than the cache [54].

**Mixture-of-experts serving moves the bottleneck from compute to weight traffic.** A survey of MoE inference optimization organizes approaches across architectural, system-level and hardware-level categories [55], and a fine-grained expert-offloading system targets the case where expert parameters exceed GPU memory and must be prefetched, reporting improved expert hit rate (summary-level evidence only) [56]. Two 2026 daily-papers entries sharpen the point: because batched decoding can touch nearly the entire expert pool, expert-weight traffic becomes the dominant bottleneck, and pruning the compute-bound prefill to save memory can sacrifice quality for little throughput benefit — motivating a scheme that runs prefill with the full model and decode with a pruned model that reuses the prefill KV cache unchanged [57]. A complementary engine positions sparsity itself as the organizing abstraction, with a shared lifecycle contract that lets each sparse method control its own KV representation and computation while coordinating with common serving infrastructure [58].

## Decoding-time acceleration

Speculative decoding established the template: an exact, lossless acceleration that computes several tokens in parallel and verifies them, without changing the output distribution [25]. The family then diverged on where the draft comes from. Medusa adds multiple decoding heads and a tree attention mechanism, reporting over 2.2× speedup with a frozen backbone (Medusa-1) and 2.3–2.8× when heads are trained jointly (Medusa-2), the latter requiring a recipe that preserves capabilities; in non-greedy settings the paper relaxes acceptance conditions, which does not guarantee lossless acceleration [4]. EAGLE instead autoregresses at the second-to-top-layer feature level, reporting 2.7–3.5× latency speedup and doubled throughput for LLaMA2-Chat 70B while maintaining the output distribution, and candidly records a practical limit: for 7B models a suitable draft model is often lacking, and using a 7B draft for a 13B target is slower than vanilla decoding [26]. EAGLE-2 makes the draft tree context-aware and reports 3.05–4.26× speedups, 20–40% faster than EAGLE-1 [59]. Lookahead decoding removes the auxiliary model entirely, trading per-step log(FLOPs) for fewer decoding steps and reporting up to 1.8× on MT-bench and 4× with strong multi-GPU scaling on code completion, while remaining compatible with memory-efficient attention [60]. LayerSkip trains for early exit with layer dropout and a shared exit loss, then verifies early exits with the remaining layers in a self-speculative loop that adds no auxiliary modules [61]. All of these numbers are author-reported; the two retrieved independent re-evaluations concern KV compression rather than speculation [13][3], and the drafted-token economics interact with the token-inflation finding discussed below [7]. At the prompt level, a 2026 system reports 21.5% fewer FLOPs with 11.4% higher accuracy on one agentic search benchmark by managing context as an explicitly maintained file rather than an append-only history [62], and a small-model-specific approach reformulates reasoning as selection from pre-specified reusable candidates rather than open-ended generation, motivated by the observation that limited model capacity can produce lengthy reasoning that provides little guidance while incurring substantial inference cost [63].

## The small-model zoo: recipes, benchmarks and what the numbers mean

The modern small-model recipe has three ingredients: aggressively filtered or synthetic data, token budgets far beyond compute-optimal, and distillation from a larger teacher. The Phi line is the clearest statement of the first ingredient. phi-1 (1.3B) was trained in four days on eight A100s on 6B tokens of filtered web data plus about 1B tokens of GPT-3.5-generated "textbook" content, reaching 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval [8]; phi-1.5 (1.3B) scaled the synthetic mixture to a 30B-token dataset and 150B training tokens, scoring 40.2 on GSM8K, with a web-augmented variant at 44.6 [64]. Phi-3-mini (3.8B) was positioned as runnable locally on a phone [65], and Phi-4 (14B) was pretrained on roughly 10T tokens, reporting MMLU 84.8, GPQA 56.1, MMLU-Pro 70.4, ArenaHard 75.4, IFEval 63.0 and a very low 3.0 on SimpleQA; it is reported to beat Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct on 9 of 12 benchmarks and to outscore its GPT-4o teacher on GPQA and MATH [66]. The 3.8B Phi-4-mini card gives the most detailed small-model accounting retrieved here (all figures as reported on the card itself, and only its parameter count was recoverable in an abstract-level re-check): 5T tokens on 512 A100-80G GPUs over 21 days, 128K context, MMLU 67.3, GSM8K 88.6 and MATH 64.0, explicitly compared against roughly twice-larger models (Qwen2.5-7B at MMLU 72.6, Gemma2-9B at 71.3, Llama-3.1-8B at 68.1) — and it also acknowledges the tradeoff, stating that the model is "fundamentally limited by its size" for factual knowledge, with retrieval proposed as the fix [67].

The second ingredient — overtraining — is visible across families. Gemma 2 trained its 2B and 9B models with knowledge distillation from the 27B teacher and reported that this improved quality even at equal token counts, with instruction-tuned Arena Elo scores of 1126 (2B, above GPT-3.5-Turbo-0613 at 1116), 1187 (9B, roughly GPT-4-0314) and 1218 (27B, above Llama-3-70B) [9]. Llama 3.2's 1B and 3B models were produced, per Meta's model card, by structured pruning from Llama 3.1 8B plus token-level logit distillation from both the 8B and 70B models, trained on up to 9T tokens and shipped with 4-bit QAT variants; the resulting instruct scores show the gap to their 8B sibling clearly (MMLU 49.3 and 63.4 vs 69.4; GSM8K 44.4 and 77.7 vs 84.5; IFEval 59.5 and 77.4 vs 80.4) even as Meta claims the 3B beats Gemma 2 2.6B and Phi-3.5-mini on instruction-following and tool-use tasks [68]. Qwen2.5 spans 0.5B–3B in the small range, with the 0.5B model at MMLU 47.5 and GSM8K 41.6 and the 3B at 65.6/79.1, and the report claims the 0.5B outperforms Gemma2-2.6B on math and coding [69]; Qwen3 continues the line at 0.6B/1.7B/4B with MMLU 52.81/62.63/72.99 and adds an adjustable "thinking budget" as an explicit latency–quality dial [70]. SmolLM trained 135M/360M/1.7B models on a curated corpus including synthetic Cosmopedia v2 (28B) and FineWeb-Edu (220B), with the tiny models on 600B tokens and the 1.7B on 1T, claiming the 1.7B outperforms Phi-1.5, MobileLM-1.5B and Qwen2-1.5B [71]; SmolLM2 pushed the 1.7B model to roughly 11T tokens with the new FineMath, Stack-Edu and SmolTalk datasets, and claims it outperforms Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama3.2-1B (summary-level evidence) [10]. TinyLlama illustrates the cost of the from-scratch route: a 1.1B model trained on about 950B tokens over roughly 90 days on 16 A100-40G GPUs to reach a commonsense average of 53.63 and MMLU 26.58 [72]. MobileLLM targets sub-billion parameters with a deep-and-thin design, embedding sharing and grouped-query attention, reporting 125M and 350M models 2.7% and 4.3% above prior same-size state of the art and 350M performance close to LLaMA-v2 7B on API calling [73]. MiniCPM (2.4B/1.2B non-embedding parameters) reports scaling-law analysis indicating a far higher data-to-model ratio than Chinchilla-optimal, uses a warmup-stable-decay scheduler for continuous training, and reaches MMLU 53.46 and HumanEval 50.0 at 2.4B [74].

Deployment-quality engineering is now as visible as pretraining recipes. Apple's on-device ~3B model combines grouped-query attention, shared embeddings, a 49K vocabulary and mixed 2/4-bit quantization averaging about 3.7 bits per weight, and reports roughly 30 tokens/s generation with about 0.6 ms time-to-first-token per prompt token on an iPhone 15 Pro, with human graders favoring it over Phi-3-mini, Mistral-7B, Gemma-7B and Llama-3-8B; small LoRA adapters (rank 16) provide task specialization [75]. An independent measurement study of 57 benchmarked SLMs found runtime memory footprints from 275MB to 2456MB, showed that vocabulary size strongly affects memory (a 250,880-token vocabulary produced a 492MB compute buffer, about 3.5× that of a comparable 1.1B model), that grouped-query attention shrinks the KV cache (164MB versus about 3.9× that for a comparable 3B model), and that Q4_K_M quantization reduced prefill latency by roughly 50% on average and decode by up to 75% [19]. Together these numbers show that architecture and deployment choices — vocabulary, attention layout, quantization format — can matter as much as parameter count for on-device viability [75][19].

Critical assessments temper the "small beats large" narrative. A controlled distillation scaling study with students and teachers from 143M to 12.6B parameters and up to 512B tokens concluded that distillation cannot beat supervised learning on cross-entropy given enough compute and data, and is only more efficient when the student's compute budget is below a student-size-dependent threshold and a teacher already exists; it also documents a capacity gap in which a stronger teacher yields a worse student [12]. A large reasoning benchmark covering 72 SLMs across 17 benchmarks found that reasoning ability is driven by training method and data quality rather than scale alone, that quantization preserves reasoning while pruning drastically disrupts it (a quantized 32B model matched full precision on one math benchmark at 55.6, while a pruned 8B model scored 0), and that larger models were more robust to adversarial perturbations [11]. The SLM survey literature reaches a compatible conclusion about definitions and open problems, offering a taxonomy of compression, pruning and quantization for small models and flagging unresolved challenges [20], and one 2026 study of same-family on-policy distillation identifies a "useful-transfer regime" in which held-out accuracy rises approximately linearly in the square root of the KL divergence for both weak-to-strong and strong-to-weak teacher–student pairs [76].

## Do the efficiency gains hold up? Scaling laws, degradation and hidden costs

The scaling-law literature is the theoretical backdrop for the small-and-overtrained strategy, and it does not speak with one voice. Kaplan-style analysis found that larger models are more sample-efficient and that compute-optimal training involves large models trained on modest data and stopped before convergence [77]. Chinchilla revised this to equal scaling of parameters and tokens, concluding that contemporary large models were significantly undertrained and that a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens outperformed much larger contemporaries — while explicitly noting that the smaller model reduces inference cost [78]. Inference-aware scaling laws then close the loop: accounting for inference demand, a practitioner expecting on the order of a billion requests should train smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal, and a study of 47 models from 150M to 6B parameters and 10 to 10,000 tokens per parameter found no evidence of a saturation point; concretely, a developer with high inference demand for a 30B-quality model could cut total FLOPs by 28% by training a 13.6B model on 2.84× the data (a figure stated in the paper's body text), and a Chinchilla-optimal 70B model costs 36% more than the cost-optimal alternative at 2T inference tokens despite only 1.3% more FLOPs [79]. The fitting methodology behind these laws is itself under scrutiny: a 2026 analysis reports that the widely used parabolic IsoFLOP approximation introduces systematic biases even on noise-free synthetic data, and estimates that applied to published Llama 3 data at a 3.8×10^25 FLOP budget the bias corresponds to a parameter underallocation worth 6.5% of the training budget and about $1.4M of unnecessary compute at 50% H100 utilization [80]. The same literature contains an unresolved disagreement about whether small models are structurally excluded from certain abilities: one influential account argues abilities emerge only above critical scales, listing thresholds such as chain-of-thought math word problems around 1.3×10^23 training FLOPs [81], while a counter-analysis attributes apparent emergence to the choice of nonlinear or discontinuous metrics and reports that the discontinuities "evaporate with different metrics or with better statistics" [82].

Behind the headline compression ratios sit measurable behavioral costs. The most direct comparison available here reports that W8A8 or W4A16 quantization can be lossless on reasoning models from 1.5B to 70B while lower bit-widths introduce significant accuracy risks, and that model size, model origin and task difficulty are the critical determinants; notably it also reports that quantized models did not exhibit increased output lengths [5]. A second study of low-bit PTQ on Qwen and LLaMA models from 0.5B to 7B reports drops of up to 69.81% on harder mathematical settings, finds that PTQ disproportionately elevates method and execution errors relative to conceptual mistakes, finds that failures emerge early with the first vulnerable step cascading to the answer, and reports that a targeted "measure → locate → restore" loop with as few as 332 curated examples recovers 4-bit math reasoning toward the full-precision baseline [6]. A third study reports that INT4/INT3 quantization can preserve accuracy while increasing reasoning-token usage, offsetting the expected per-token speedup, and recommends reporting reasoning-token usage alongside accuracy — a direct contradiction of the null result above, and a reminder that these effects are configuration- and task-dependent [7]. The energy accounting literature shows a structurally similar trap: because serving is priced per token but GPUs consume energy over inference windows, per-token energy can fall while total request energy rises. On Llama-3.2-1B on an H200 at batch 16 and 4K context, increasing output length from 10 to 512 tokens reduced token energy from 7.46 to 0.72 J/token while total batched inference-window energy rose from 1.19 to 5.93 kJ, and the batch-16 over batch-1 gain fell from 6.31× at 512 context to 1.17× at 4K context [14].

## Energy, carbon and the economics of where to run a small model

Energy and carbon accounting for LLMs began with training-only tools. The ML emissions calculator takes region, hardware and training time as inputs and quantifies grid variability — within North America servers can emit from about 20 gCO2eq/kWh in Quebec to 736.6 gCO2eq/kWh in Iowa — but its authors explicitly note it does not cover inference deployment or datacenter PUE [83]. LLMCarbon extends the scope to training, inference, experimentation and storage including embodied carbon, and critiques the earlier tool for being unable to model dense or MoE LLMs and for ignoring embodied footprints [84]. Direct measurements complicate the intuitive story that local inference is greener. A benchmark on an Apple M4 Pro with 48GB unified memory measured 0.47 W of CPU+GPU package power during sustained inference with 8–12 W total system power, reporting 30–40× better energy efficiency per token than datacenter GPUs in single-user deployment, and found that 3–3.8B models delivered 2.6–4.2× higher throughput and up to 62% less energy per token than 7–9B models, with a 7B model at 57% MMLU and 59 tokens/s as the accuracy–efficiency compromise and a 3B model at 175 tokens/s for latency-critical use [85]. A mobile study covering 18 model configurations on two smartphones and a server reaches the opposite headline: on-device inference was on average 3× less energy-efficient than batched server inference, the relationship between quantization bit-width and energy per token was non-monotonic with energy "sweet spots" on both phones, and 88–90% of the local-inference impact was attributable to device embodied carbon rather than electricity — leading the authors to challenge "the premise that local AI is more sustainable than cloud inference" [86]. The two results are compatible only because they compare against different baselines: one measures single-user deployment against datacenter GPUs, the other batched servers against phones including embodied device cost [85][86]. Hardware co-design pushes the same frontier from below: an in-memory-computing accelerator aimed at the memory-bound GEMV decoding regime reports up to 7.3× higher throughput and 49.59× better energy efficiency than an NVIDIA Orin Nano on LLaMA3.2-1B, 9.95× higher throughput than a Qualcomm part on the 3B model, and average INT4 results of 336.42 tokens/s and 173.02 tokens/J across a dozen small models — with the important caveat that these are simulated plus a 65nm macro, not a fabricated product [15].

Where the model runs also determines whether it is competent for the task. A 2026 study of small models as local agent controllers found that a 1.5B model alone achieved 67.3% task success on a 52-task tool-use split, while uncertainty-aware step-level escalation to a 7B backend reached 82.7% success using 30.8% cloud actions; in multi-turn evaluation, escalation achieved 69.0% trajectory and 84.0% action success using only 30.0% cloud actions, against 48.0%/70.5% for local-only execution, with a strong-only backend at 82.0% trajectory success and 100% cloud actions [87]. The authors' own limitations are unusually explicit — one model family, one stronger backend, scripted tasks, small test sets, and risk tiers described as research annotations rather than safety guarantees [87]. The consistent picture across compression, serving, decoding, energy and agent work is that the interesting question is no longer whether small models can be compressed and served cheaply, but which combination of compression level, runtime and routing policy achieves a target quality under a measured latency and energy budget [3][5][86][87].

## Trends and open problems

**Phase-aware optimization is the strongest convergent trend.** Chunked prefill and prefill/decode disaggregation [2][24], phase-specific expert pruning that keeps the full model for prefill and a pruned model for decode [57], prefill-only sparse attention [52], prefill-only prefix caching [48] and prefill-oriented prompt reuse [47] all reflect the same insight: prefill and decode have different bottlenecks, so a single global compression or scheduling policy is inherently suboptimal. Decoding-level acceleration is a separate economy again, where the relevant question is not FLOPs but tokens drafted per verified step and the overhead of the drafting mechanism [4][26][60][61].

**Measurement is the weakest link.** Most efficiency claims in this literature are author-reported and measured against a specific baseline, and the retrieved evidence includes vendor figures without disclosed hardware [44], speedups that differ between preprint and camera-ready versions [28], and lossless-acceleration claims whose validity depends on greedy versus non-greedy decoding [4][59]. Independent re-evaluations find that memory savings do not reliably become throughput or latency gains [3], that compression can cause instructions to be effectively ignored in multi-instruction settings [13], that chunked compression creates position-dependent weak spots [53], and that per-token energy metrics are structurally incomplete without request-level accounting [14]. The most actionable open problem is a reporting standard: accuracy together with output length and token usage [7], per-sample rather than aggregate quality [3], and end-to-end request-level latency, throughput and energy rather than component-level savings [3][14]. This survey's own verification pass illustrates the asymmetry: of ten claims re-fetched against their cited sources, six were confirmed at abstract level, while four — the GPTQ perplexity comparison, the Phi-4-mini and Llama 3.2 model-card figures, and a Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal FLOPs example — rested on full-text or model-card passages that a truncated re-fetch could recover only in part, so they are presented here as source-reported rather than independently re-verified.

**Quality risks from compression are real but task- and recipe-dependent, and the literature openly disagrees.** Lossless W4A16 versus catastrophic low-bit math failure [5][6], token inflation versus no output-length change [5][7], quantization preserving reasoning while pruning destroys it [11] — these are not reconciliable by a single number, and none of the retrieved studies spans enough models, bit-widths and task families to settle them. Longer-term architectural shifts may change the target: quantization of recurrent states in linear-attention models [54] addresses a different bottleneck than KV-cache compression, and sparsity-first serving engines suggest that cache representation should be a pluggable, method-specific design decision rather than a fixed engine invariant [58].

**The small-model frontier is now data-, distillation- and deployment-bound rather than scale-bound.** The strongest evidence for this is that distillation's advantage is conditional and bounded [12], that reasoning ability in small models tracks training method and data quality rather than size [11], and that distillation scaling behaves measurably differently in weak-to-strong, same-base and strong-to-weak directions [76]. Model cards and reports confirm the practical limits: small models explicitly concede factual-knowledge ceilings that motivate retrieval [67], and their benchmark gaps against their own larger siblings remain visible [68]. The engineering frontier has moved to vocabulary size, attention layout, quantization format and adapter-based specialization [75][19], to structured low-rank compression that reasons about error propagation rather than local reconstruction error [88], and to selecting reasoning structure instead of generating it when model capacity is the constraint [63]. Two methodological problems remain open: the definition of "small" keeps drifting with device memory [19][20], which undermines cross-study comparison, and the scaling-law fits used to justify token budgets carry systematic biases that are large enough to matter in absolute compute and dollars [80]. Finally, energy and sustainability claims should be treated as configuration statements, not general truths, until baselines (single-user versus batched, operational versus embodied) are stated explicitly [83][84][85][86].
